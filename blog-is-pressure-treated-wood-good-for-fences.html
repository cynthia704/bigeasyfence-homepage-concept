Is Pressure-Treated Wood Good for Fences?

Quick Summary
  • Pressure-treated pine is a solid fence wood for most Louisiana yards, offering rot and insect resistance at a lower price than most alternatives.
  • Look for lumber rated for ground contact on posts, and let new boards dry before staining, since fresh treated wood is often wet.
  • Treated wood still needs cleaning, sealing, and regular checks, because treatment slows decay but does not stop it.
  • Wear gloves and eye protection when cutting, wash up afterward, and never burn treated scraps.

For most yards in the New Orleans area, yes. Pressure-treated pine is the workhorse fence wood in this region, and it earned that spot with a good balance of price and staying power. The longer answer covers what "treated" really means, which grade to buy, and the care it needs to reach its full life.

This guide from Big Easy Fence is a buyer's and owner's checklist for a treated wood fence. It covers how the treatment works, where treated lumber falls short, what to check before you order, and how to keep the fence in shape.

What Pressure Treatment Actually Does

Pressure treatment loads lumber into a sealed chamber and forces preservatives deep into the wood fibers. Those preservatives protect against rot, fungus, and wood-eating insects such as termites, which is a real advantage in a warm, damp climate. Southern yellow pine is the usual base wood because it absorbs the treatment well.

Today's residential lumber generally uses copper-based preservatives such as ACQ and copper azole. The older arsenic-based CCA treatment has been phased out for most home uses. The protection is strong, but it is not a force field. Treated wood is not waterproof, and it can still warp, split, and gray if it is left unfinished.

Strengths and Limits of Treated Wood

What It Does Well

  • Resists rot and insects. The preservatives protect the wood from the inside, which matters most in soil contact.
  • Costs less. Treated pine is usually among the lowest-priced wood options.
  • Takes finishes. Once it dries, it accepts stain, sealer, and paint.
  • Is easy to find. Suppliers stock standard sizes, so parts and repairs are simple to match.

Where It Falls Short

  • Moves as it dries. Fresh treated lumber holds a lot of water and can twist, shrink, or crack as it dries.
  • Needs sealing. Without a finish, boards gray and check in the sun.
  • Has exposed cut ends. Every cut leaves untreated wood that needs a brush-on preservative.
  • Corrodes some metals. The copper in the wood attacks plain steel fasteners over time.

What to Check When Buying Treated Lumber

Two boards that look alike at the yard can be treated to very different levels. A few quick checks make a big difference in how long the fence lasts.

Ground-Contact Rating for Posts

Lumber is treated to different levels depending on where it will be used. Posts set in soil need lumber rated for ground contact, which carries more preservative than lumber meant for above-ground use. The rating appears on the end tag stapled to the board, so read it before you buy.

Moisture Content

Lumber that just came out of treatment feels heavy and damp. Buying boards that have had time to dry, or letting them sit before installing and finishing, reduces warping and shrinkage afterward.

Fasteners and Hardware

Use screws, nails, and brackets rated for treated lumber, typically hot-dipped galvanized or stainless. Plain steel can corrode, leaving streaks and weak connections in a few seasons.

Board Quality

Pick straight boards with fewer large knots. Boards that are bowed at the yard tend to stay that way. If you are ordering a whole fence, ask your supplier how they sort and store their lumber.

Common Fence Styles Built With Treated Pine

Treated pine works with almost any wood fence style. These four are among the most popular.

Full Privacy

Board-on-Board Privacy

Overlapping vertical boards block sightlines while still letting some air through the gaps.

Classic

Picket Fence

Low, evenly spaced pickets mark a boundary and add curb appeal. Each board is a small surface to seal.

Airflow

Shadowbox

Boards alternate on both sides of the rails, giving privacy from an angle and letting wind and light pass through.

Decorative

Lattice Top

A solid lower fence with a lattice band on top adds height and a lighter look for gardens and patios.

Caring for a Treated Wood Fence

A short routine goes a long way. Most of it takes a weekend once a year.

  1. Clean it. Wash away dirt, mildew, and algae with mild soap and a soft brush, then rinse well.
  2. Let it dry. New treated wood needs time to dry before a finish will bond. A simple test is to sprinkle water on a board. If it soaks in, the wood is ready, and if it beads up, wait longer.
  3. Seal or stain it. A water-repellent finish with UV protection slows warping and graying. Our guide to choosing the best wood stain for fences covers transparent and solid finishes.
  4. Inspect it. Look for loose fasteners, soft spots, leaning posts, and areas where water collects near drains and gutters.
  5. Repair promptly. Replace damaged boards with the same grade of treated lumber so the whole fence stays protected.
Handle Treated Wood Safely

Wear gloves, eye protection, and a dust mask when cutting, and wash your hands afterward. Never burn treated scraps, and dispose of offcuts according to local guidelines.

When to Bring in a Fence Company

Treated lumber is forgiving, but a fence is only as good as its posts and layout. If you are planning a long run, a gate, or a sloped yard, or you simply want the lumber grade and fasteners chosen for you, a professional installer can handle it.
