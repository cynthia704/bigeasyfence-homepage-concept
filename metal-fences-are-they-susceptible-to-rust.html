Are Metal Fences Susceptible to Rust?

Metal Fence Guide5 min readBy Big Easy Fence

Quick Summary
  • Rust needs iron. Steel and iron fences rust when their coating fails, and aluminum does not rust because it contains no iron.
  • Aluminum can still corrode or pit in harsh conditions, and its powder-coated finish keeps it looking good.
  • In humid Louisiana, coatings do most of the work: powder coating, galvanizing, and paint keep water away from the metal underneath.
  • Wash the fence, touch up chips early, and repair spots before rust spreads into posts, welds, and gate hinges.

The short answer is that it depends on the metal. Steel and iron fences rust when their protective finish fails. Aluminum does not rust at all, though it has its own weak points. Coatings matter for all of them, and Louisiana's humid climate tests those coatings every day.

Here is how rust works, how each metal behaves, and what to do about it. If you are choosing a metal fence, Big Easy Fence can help match the metal to your yard, and this guide will help you ask the right questions.

What Rust Actually Is

Rust is iron reacting with oxygen and water. It forms a flaky orange-brown layer that does not protect the metal beneath it, so the damage keeps going as long as moisture and air keep reaching the iron.

That is the whole reason some fence metals rust and others do not. If a metal contains no iron, it cannot rust. If it does contain iron, its coating is the only thing standing between the metal and the weather.

Metal by Metal

Each fence metal handles moisture in its own way.

Aluminum

Aluminum contains no iron, so it does not rust. It forms a thin, tight oxide layer on its surface that slows further attack. It can still corrode in tough conditions, showing up as white powder or small pits, especially where salt air, standing water, or contact with steel fasteners are involved. A powder-coated finish protects both the color and the metal. See the aluminum fence guide for more.

Steel

Steel is mostly iron, so bare steel rusts. Steel fences are protected by paint, powder coating, or galvanizing. While that coating is intact, steel holds up well. Once water reaches bare steel through a chip, scratch, or worn edge, rust can start.

Galvanized Steel

Galvanized steel is coated with zinc. The zinc shields the steel underneath and slows rust, including near small scratches. Over time, the zinc layer wears down, and it wears faster in constant moisture or salty air. Many galvanized fences are also painted or powder-coated for a longer-lasting finish.

Wrought and Cast Iron

Iron rusts readily without a finish. Iron fences rely on paint or powder coating and need touch-ups. Much of what is sold today as wrought iron is actually mild steel fabricated by welding, so it behaves like painted steel.

Stainless Steel

Stainless steel contains chromium, which forms a protective surface layer. It resists rust far better than plain steel, though it can still develop stains or surface rust in salty or heavily polluted air. It is uncommon for residential fences.

Resistant Does Not Mean Immune

Every metal fence has a weak point. For steel and iron it is the coating. For aluminum it is the finish and the fasteners. Ask which finish is used and how it is applied.

Why Coatings Matter

On a steel or iron fence, the coating is the fence's real defense. Once it is compromised, the metal is exposed.

  • Powder coating. A baked-on dry powder finish that is thicker and tougher than ordinary paint. Chips and deep scratches still need touch-ups.
  • Galvanizing. A zinc layer applied to steel. It gives base-level protection and can be painted over.
  • Paint and primer. Rust-inhibiting primer with an outdoor metal topcoat is the standard field repair, and it is how many iron fences are maintained.

Weak spots on any coated fence include weld points, cut edges, the bottoms of posts and pickets, and the places where hardware attaches. Water collects there first, so those are the spots to inspect.

What Speeds Rust Up in Louisiana

Not every yard puts the same stress on a fence. These conditions do the most damage.

Humidity and Heavy Rain

Metal in this region stays damp for long stretches. Moisture that sits on a chipped surface is what sets rust in motion.

Salt Air

Salty air near the coast or wetlands speeds up corrosion on most metals. If your property is close to open water, ask a contractor about finishes suited to that exposure.

Standing Water and Sprinklers

Fence bottoms that sit in wet ground, or in the spray of a sprinkler head, rust faster than the rest. Aim sprinklers away from the fence and keep the soil drained.

Scratches, Dense Plants, and Trapped Debris

Mower nicks and weed trimmer strikes chip the finish. Vines and shrubs pressed against the fence hold moisture against it. Trim plants back so air can move.

Spotting and Fixing Small Rust Spots

Catch rust while it is small and it is a quick job. Check the fence a couple of times a year and after major storms, and look closely at the bottoms of posts and pickets, welds, and gate hinges.

  1. Clean the area. Remove dirt and loose paint from the spot and just beyond it.
  2. Remove loose rust. Use a wire brush or sandpaper until you reach solid metal. Wear eye protection and gloves.
  3. Treat and prime. Apply a rust-inhibiting primer, or a rust converter if the product label calls for one.
  4. Repaint. Finish with paint made for outdoor metal, and let it dry fully.

Follow the instructions on each product, work in good ventilation, and protect nearby plants and surfaces from drips.

When Rust Needs a Professional

Surface rust is a maintenance job. Rust that has flaked away metal, formed holes, or spread into welds and post bases is a repair job. Loose pickets, sagging gates, and cracked welds are signs the structure has been weakened. A crew can cut out damaged sections, replace parts, and refinish the fence properly.
