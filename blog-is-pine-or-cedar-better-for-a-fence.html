Is Pine or Cedar Better for a Fence?

Quick Summary
  • Treated pine costs less at install, and cedar costs more per board, so the first question is how long you plan to keep the fence.
  • Over ten years, upkeep, repairs, and replacement can narrow or reverse the gap, depending on how the fence is cared for.
  • Untreated pine is cheap but breaks down quickly in the ground, so posts and boards should be treated or rated for soil contact.
  • Pine suits long runs and tight budgets, while cedar suits visible fences and owners who want a natural look.

Price is what sends most people to this question. Pine costs less at the register, and cedar costs more. What matters is what each one costs by year ten, once finishing, repairs, and replacement are counted.

This guide from Big Easy Fence looks at pine and cedar through the budget lens. It covers the upfront gap, the long-term costs that shift the math, and the situations where each wood is the smarter buy for a wood fence in south Louisiana. In this region, "pine" almost always means pressure-treated Southern yellow pine, so that is what we compare here.

Upfront Cost: What You Pay at Install

Treated pine is one of the lowest-priced fence woods you can buy. It is grown in large volume, milled to standard sizes, and easy to find. Cedar is priced higher per board, and the better grades with fewer knots cost more still. On a long fence, that difference adds up quickly.

Labor is a smaller factor than most people expect. The cost of installing a fence depends more on the length, the height, the ground, and the number of gates than on which wood is going in. The gap between the two woods shows up mainly in the lumber bill.

Long-Term Cost: Upkeep, Repairs, and Replacement

The price at install is only the first payment. These are the costs that follow.

Finishing and Sealing

Both woods weather in our sun and rain. Treated pine needs a sealer or stain to slow warping, checking, and gray fading, and that recoat comes around every couple of years. Cedar can go without a finish, and many owners let it turn silver-gray. Sealing cedar keeps the warm color and slows cracking, so it is worth budgeting for if looks matter to you.

Repairs

Pine boards tend to warp and split as they dry and weather. Cedar is softer, so it dents and can split near fasteners. In both cases, small repairs done early cost far less than a rebuild.

Replacement

Both woods commonly reach roughly 15 to 20 years or more, with cedar sometimes going a bit longer when it is well cared for. If the fence is going to be replaced at about the same point either way, the cheaper wood keeps its lead. If cedar buys you extra years, its price starts to look better.

Budget for the Right Fasteners

Cedar's natural compounds and the copper in treated lumber both corrode plain steel. Stainless or coated screws and nails cost a little more, but they keep stains and failed connections from adding to the bill later.

A Word on Untreated Pine

Untreated pine is the cheapest board on the shelf, and that price is tempting. It is also the least durable. Without treatment, pine rots and attracts termites quickly in wet soil and humid air. If a fence quote looks much lower than the rest, ask whether the pine is pressure-treated and whether the posts are rated for ground contact. A fence that needs replacing after a few years is not a bargain.

When Pine Is the Smarter Buy

  • Long fence lines. Savings per board multiply across a big yard or a full perimeter.
  • Painted or solid-stained fences. If the finish covers the grain anyway, paying for cedar's look adds little.
  • Back and side yards. Areas that guests never see do not need a premium appearance.
  • Short-term plans. If you may sell or rebuild in several years, a lower price is easier to justify.

When Cedar Earns Its Price

  • Front-facing fences. Cedar's fine grain and warm color show best where everyone sees them.
  • Clear or light finishes. Cedar takes transparent stain well, and the wood grain becomes the feature.
  • Long-term ownership. If you plan to stay for years, extra board life can offset part of the higher price.
  • Lower-fuss upkeep. Cedar tolerates a lighter maintenance routine, though it still benefits from cleaning and periodic sealing.

Ways to Trim the Bill With Either Wood

Whichever wood you choose, a few decisions lower the total without weakening the fence. Keep the height and style simple, since decorative tops and custom cuts add lumber and labor. Fence only the areas that need it. Use treated lumber for posts even if the boards are cedar, since posts sit in wet ground. And read up on ways to save money when buying a fence before you compare quotes.

A Simple Way to Decide

Ask three questions. How long will you own the property? How much of the fence will be seen? How much upkeep will you actually do? Short horizon, low visibility, and a tight budget point to treated pine. Long horizon, high visibility, and a wish for a natural finish point to cedar.
