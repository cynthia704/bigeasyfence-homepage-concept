What to Look for in a New Orleans Fence Contractor?

Quick Summary
  • Build a short list from neighbors, reviews, and finished projects, then compare at least two or three written estimates line by line.
  • Verify license and insurance yourself with the state and your city or parish instead of relying on a contractor saying so.
  • Ask about permits, utilities, slopes, materials, crew, and timeline before you sign, and get the answers in writing.
  • Read the contract and any warranty terms closely, and be wary of pressure, vague quotes, or a demand for full payment up front.

A fence is a big outdoor project, and the contractor matters as much as the material. Two crews can quote the same fence and deliver very different results. This checklist covers what to verify, what to ask, and what to put in writing before you hire anyone in the New Orleans area.

Whether you talk to a local fence company or a general contractor, the same questions apply. Doing this homework takes a few evenings, and it can save you from a leaning gate, a surprise bill, or a fence built where it should not be.

Build a Short List You Can Trust

Start by collecting three to five names. You want a list of companies with a track record you can actually see, so skip the habit of calling whoever shows up first in a search.

Ask Neighbors and Friends

The best first-hand report is from someone whose fence has been through a few New Orleans summers and at least one storm season. Ask what went well, what went wrong, and whether they would hire the same crew again. A fence that still looks straight after a hard rain says more than a sales pitch.

Read Reviews and Check for Complaints

Look at online reviews with a critical eye. A handful of detailed reviews, good and bad, tells you more than a perfect score with no comments. You can also check the Better Business Bureau for complaints and see how the company responded to them. Pay attention to patterns, such as repeated mentions of missed appointments or unfinished cleanup.

Look at Finished Work

Ask to see photos of completed jobs similar to yours, and browse a company's project gallery if it has one. Look for straight lines, consistent spacing, gates that swing and latch cleanly, and tidy connections at posts. If you can visit an older installation in person, even better.

Verify Credentials Before You Sign

Licensing and insurance are where a lot of homeowners take a contractor at their word. Do not. Requirements can differ by the type of work, the size of the job, and the city or parish where the fence goes up, so check with the state and with your local permitting office to learn what applies to your project.

  • License. Ask for the license number and confirm it with the issuing agency yourself, rather than trusting a printed claim.
  • General liability insurance. This helps cover damage to your property or a neighbor's if something goes wrong on the job.
  • Workers' compensation. Ask how crew members are covered, so an injury on your property does not become your problem.
  • Proof, not promises. Ask for a certificate of insurance and, if you want to be thorough, have it sent to you directly by the insurer.
Write Down Who You Talked To

Keep a simple note of the name, date, and answer for each check. If a question comes up later, you will have a record to point to.

Get More Than One Written Estimate

Collect at least two or three estimates for the same fence, with the same height, material, and gate count. That is the only way to compare them fairly. A low number that leaves out something you assumed was included is not a bargain.

A useful estimate lists the materials, the total footage, the number and type of gates, removal of any old fence, and what is included for cleanup. If a quote is a single line with a total, ask for more detail.

Costs vary for reasons that have nothing to do with the contractor. A large lot with mature trees, a sloped yard, uneven ground, or a tight access route can all add time and materials. Height and material change the price too. Ask each company to explain what in your yard is driving its number, and you will spot the estimates that skipped a problem the others found.

Questions to Ask During the Site Visit

A site visit is your chance to see how a company thinks. Have a clear idea of the fence you want, and be ready with the approximate property lines, the gate locations, and any trouble spots such as low areas or big roots. Then ask questions like these.

Permits and Property Lines

Ask who is responsible for permits, who pays for them, and whether any extra fees should be expected. Rules on height, placement, and materials come from your city or parish and sometimes from a homeowners association, so confirm them rather than assuming. If you are not sure where your property line is, a survey will settle it before posts go in the ground. Our guide on permits for a fence in your yard covers what to check.

Utilities and Digging

Post holes mean digging, and buried lines are a real hazard. Ask how the crew handles utility locating before work starts. In the US, 811 is the number used to request marking of underground lines, and it is worth knowing who will make that call.

Materials and Installation

Ask what the fence is made of, how the posts are set, and how gates are supported. In Louisiana, heavy rain, humidity, termites, and storm winds all put stress on a fence, so material and post setting matter. Wood, vinyl, chain link, aluminum, and iron each age differently. A contractor who knows the local conditions will explain the trade-offs without pushing one option. Our overview of wood fences shows what a typical build involves.

Crew and Timeline

Find out who will actually do the work, whether that is the company's own crew or a subcontractor, and who your point of contact will be. Ask for a start window and an expected finish, and ask what happens if rain or a storm pushes the schedule. Timing depends on footage, material, permits, and weather. Our note on the best month for fence installation explains how the seasons affect scheduling in this area.

Read the Contract, Warranty, and Payment Terms

Everything you discussed should end up on paper. A contract protects both sides, and a reputable company will not object to putting details in writing.

The Contract

Check that it names the materials, the fence height, the gate details, the scope of work, the start and completion window, and what is included for removal and cleanup. If something was promised out loud, it belongs in the contract.

The Warranty

Ask whether workmanship and materials are covered, for how long, and what is not covered. Get the warranty in writing, and ask what you need to do to keep it valid, such as sealing wood or maintaining a finish. Terms differ from one company and one material to another, so read the fine print instead of comparing headline promises.

Payment

Be cautious about any request to pay the full amount before work starts. Ask how payments are structured, when each is due, and what triggers it. A schedule tied to progress, with the final payment due after you have walked the finished fence, keeps everyone aligned. Hold the last payment until any small fixes you spotted on that walk-through are done.

Red Flags Worth Walking Away From

Most problems show up before the job starts. If you notice any of these, slow down and ask more questions.

  • Pressure to decide today. A fair quote will still be there after you have compared it.
  • A vague or verbal-only estimate. If they will not put it in writing, that is a reason to pause.
  • No proof of insurance or a license number. Anyone who dodges a simple request is telling you something.
  • Skipping permits or property lines. A fence built in the wrong place or without approval can end up costing far more to fix.
  • A demand for all the money up front. Payment should follow progress on the job.
  • Hard to reach. If replies are slow now, they will be slower once you have paid a deposit.

Making the Final Call

Line up your estimates, your notes on each company, and the answers you were given. The right choice is usually the one that was clear, patient, and specific, not automatically the cheapest or the most polished. If two are close, go with the one you found easiest to talk to, since you will be working with them through the whole project.
