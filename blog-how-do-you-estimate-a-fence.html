How Do You Estimate a Fence? New Orleans Guide & Tips

Quick Summary
  • Every fence estimate starts with linear feet, so measure the exact run you plan to fence and decide on a height and style.
  • Materials, gates, hardware, terrain, access, and labor all add to the total, and each one belongs on its own line of a written quote.
  • The basic formula is total linear feet times the per-foot rate, plus gates and extras, and a worked example below shows the arithmetic.
  • Confirm permit and property-line rules for your address before you lock in the plan, and compare written quotes line by line.

A good fence estimate is less mysterious than it sounds. It is a measurement, a list of parts, a few adjustments for your yard, and the cost of the labor to put it all together. Once you see the pieces, a quote stops being a black box and you can spot the ones that leave something out.

This guide from Big Easy Fence walks through how a fence project gets estimated for a New Orleans property, whether you are checking a contractor's number or planning a residential fence on your own.

Step 1: Measure the Linear Feet

A linear foot is one foot of fence along a straight line. Fence pricing and materials are measured this way, so every estimate starts with the total length you want to enclose.

Walk the line where the fence will go and measure each straight run. For a rectangle or square, add the length of each side you plan to fence. Skip any side that already has a wall or an existing fence, and note the width of every gate opening.

A Worked Example

Say your yard is 50 feet wide and 80 feet deep, and you want a fence across the back and down both sides, with the house closing off the front. That is 50 + 80 + 80, or 210 linear feet.

Posts usually sit about 6 to 8 feet apart, depending on the style. At 8-foot spacing, 210 feet is about 27 sections and 28 posts. At 6-foot spacing, it is 35 sections and 36 posts. The closer spacing means more posts and more concrete, which changes the price.

Step 2: Choose the Height and Style

Height and style decide how much material each linear foot needs. A taller fence uses more boards or panels, and a solid privacy fence uses more than a picket or split rail. The type of material comes next, and our fence types page helps you compare wood, vinyl, metal, and chain link before you commit.

Step 4: Adjust for Your Property

The same fence costs more on a difficult lot. Tell every contractor about the conditions below so their numbers reflect them.

  • Slope and uneven ground. Sloped runs take more cutting, stepping, and labor.
  • Trees, roots, and obstacles. Anything in the path adds time, and sometimes a change to the layout.
  • Access. Tight side yards make it harder to move materials and equipment.
  • Soil and drainage. Where water collects, posts need more care to keep them from rotting or shifting.
  • Utilities. Underground lines must be located and marked before anyone digs. Call 811, the national one-call number, before the work starts.
Ask for Line Items in Writing

A quote with one lump-sum number hides what is and is not included. Ask for posts, materials, gates, labor, removal, and finish listed separately, and keep a copy so you can compare later.

Step 5: Work Out the Labor

Labor depends on the length of the fence, the style, and the difficulty of the site. It usually appears as its own line, so you can see what you are paying for installation. Ask each contractor to break it out.

Doing the job yourself can cut the labor line, but it swaps that cost for tools, rental fees, and your time. A mistake in post spacing or alignment can cost more to fix than you saved, so weigh it honestly.

Step 6: Check Permits and Property Lines

New Orleans has its own permit process and zoning rules for fences, and some neighborhoods add design guidelines. Confirm the requirements for your address before you finalize the plan, and make sure you know exactly where your property line runs. A fence built in the wrong spot is an expensive lesson.

Putting the Estimate Together

The basic formula is simple: total linear feet times the per-foot rate for your chosen material and height, plus gates, extras, and any site adjustments. For a rough idea of what per-foot rates look like, see our post on the average cost of a fence per foot.

Get at least two or three written quotes, and compare them line by line, not by the bottom number alone. If one quote is far lower, check what it leaves out. When you are ready for a real number on your own yard, ask for a free estimate and we will measure it with you.

Common Questions

How Do You Estimate a Fence?
Measure the total linear feet you need, choose a height and style, and list the materials, gates, and hardware. Then add labor and any site adjustments, and confirm permit and property-line rules. Multiply the linear feet by the per-foot rate for the material, then add gates and extras.
How Do I Estimate the Cost of a Wood Fence?
Count the linear feet, then price the posts, rails, pickets, hardware, and finish. Multiply the per-foot figure by your total length and add labor. The wood species, height, and style change the number, so ask for a quote for each option.
How Much Is 300 Feet of Fence?
It depends on the material, height, and labor. Multiply 300 by the per-foot rate you are quoted, then add gates and extras. Two quotes for the same 300 feet can differ a lot if they assume different materials, so compare the line items.
How Do You Calculate Linear Feet and Acreage of a Fence?
For a rectangle, add up the lengths of the sides you will fence to get the linear feet. To find the acreage inside it, multiply the length by the width in feet and divide by 43,560, the number of square feet in an acre.
